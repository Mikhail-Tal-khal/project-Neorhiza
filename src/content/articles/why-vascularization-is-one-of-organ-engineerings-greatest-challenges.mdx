Of all the obstacles between today's tissue models and tomorrow's functional tissue, transport is the one that quietly decides everything. Small constructs can be remarkable — but every cell still needs a supply of oxygen and nutrients.

## Why supply matters

In natural tissue, a dense network of small vessels runs close to nearly every cell, delivering oxygen and nutrients and carrying waste away. Cells cut off from that supply quickly struggle.

This is why thick engineered tissue, left without transport support, develops a familiar failure pattern: a viable rim of cells around a starving, dying core. Researchers know this pattern well. It is the wall the field has been trying to climb for decades.

## What research has tried

The scientific literature describes many approaches to this problem. Each teaches something. None has yet produced the gold standard: stable, connected, appropriately patterned vessel networks that mature with the tissue they serve — at relevant scales, with consistency.

This is not a criticism of the field. It is an accurate description of an unsolved problem that excellent laboratories around the world are working on with rigor and patience.

## Neorhiza's first question

Project Neorhiza's first proposed program asks a deliberately narrow, measurable question in this area: can controlled perfusion improve the sustained function and viability of a small multicellular liver-tissue construct compared with an equivalent non-perfused construct?

We publish no protocols, parameters or specifications here — those remain confidential and subject to institutional review. But the shape of the question is public, and the shape of the question is the point: in tissue engineering, transport is not a detail. It is the difference between a model that survives and one that matters.
