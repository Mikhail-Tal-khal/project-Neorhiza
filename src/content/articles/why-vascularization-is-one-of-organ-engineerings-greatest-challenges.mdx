Of all the obstacles between today's tissue models and tomorrow's functional tissue, transport is the one that quietly decides everything. You can grow remarkable small constructs in a dish — but only as long as every cell remains close enough to a supply of oxygen and nutrients.

## The physics of being alive

In natural tissue, capillaries run within roughly tens to hundreds of micrometers of nearly every cell. Diffusion — the passive movement of molecules — is wonderfully effective over those distances and hopeless beyond them. Double the distance a cell sits from a supply line, and its oxygen delivery does not halve; it collapses.

This is why thick engineered tissue, left without transport support, develops a familiar failure pattern: a viable rim of cells around a starving, dying core. Researchers know this pattern well. It is the wall the field has been trying to climb for decades.

## What research has tried

The scientific literature describes many approaches: seeding vessel-associated cells that might form networks, printing channel structures into supportive materials, growing constructs around pre-made vascular-like templates, and applying fluid flow through and around developing tissue. Each approach teaches something. None has yet produced the gold standard: stable, connected, appropriately patterned vessel networks that mature with the tissue they serve — at relevant scales, with consistency.

This is not a criticism of the field. It is an accurate description of an unsolved problem that excellent laboratories around the world are working on with rigor and patience.

## Why perfusion matters

One promising research direction asks a focused question: does controlled perfusion — moving culture medium through a developing construct in a defined way — improve its sustained function and viability compared with an equivalent construct left un-perfused? The question is deliberately narrow, measurable and answerable. It is also, conveniently, the exact question Project Neorhiza proposes to study first in its small liver-tissue research model.

We publish no protocols, parameters or specifications here — those remain confidential and subject to institutional review. But the shape of the question is public, and the shape of the question is the point: in tissue engineering, transport is not a detail. It is the difference between a model that survives and one that matters.
