It is a common and understandable intuition: DNA contains the blueprint of life, so perhaps a sufficient copy of the right blueprint could simply be "grown" into an organ. The science says otherwise — and the reasons are illuminating.

## Instructions versus builders

DNA is best understood as a library of instructions that **cells** read, interpret and act upon. A genome outside of a cell does nothing: it cannot metabolize, respond to signals, divide or organize. All of the activity we associate with life is performed by living cells executing genetic programs within a structured environment.

Developmental biology has shown, over more than a century, that an organ emerges from the coordinated behavior of enormous numbers of cells — dividing, migrating, specializing and communicating — inside a three-dimensional, signal-rich setting. The instructions matter enormously, but they are only one part of a system whose other parts include the cells themselves, their physical surroundings, mechanical forces, chemical gradients and time.

## What this means for regenerative medicine

If instructions alone built organs, regenerative medicine would be a computational problem. Because living cells do the building, regenerative medicine is primarily a **biological and engineering** problem: how to obtain the right cells, guide them toward the right identities, give them a supportive three-dimensional environment, supply them with oxygen and nutrients, and evaluate what they construct.

This is why the scientific pathway that Project Neorhiza describes begins with living cells — ethically obtained and handled under supervision — rather than with abstract genetic information. The pathway runs from cells, through reprogramming and specialization, toward organization, transport and maturation, and ends with rigorous functional and safety evaluation.

## An honest boundary

No method currently exists to turn a genome by itself into organized living tissue, and none is on the horizon in the way popular imagination sometimes suggests. Claims that imply otherwise deserve skepticism. The field's real promise lies in a different, more interesting direction: understanding how cells build tissue, and whether that knowledge can eventually be applied, safely and under strict oversight, to medicine that genuinely helps patients.

That is the future Project Neorhiza finds worth working toward — carefully, and without shortcuts.
